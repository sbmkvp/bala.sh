Day 8 of the [#30DayMapChallenge](https://30daymapchallenge.com/2026/) 2026.

> **Utopia** — A map of something which could be.

On Day 7, one of my 23 quick maps showed NSW's projected unmet housing demand: **320,900 dwellings by 2041**.
The LGA at the top of that list was **Canterbury-Bankstown, at 23,600**.

So my utopia for Day 8 is a simple one: **what if Canterbury-Bankstown could fix its housing shortfall just by building near its train stations?**

![MapAI showing 800-metre catchments around 18 railway stations in Canterbury-Bankstown, shaded light to dark by potential dwellings, with Bankstown, Campsie and Canterbury darkest, and a results panel listing a total of 766,524 potential dwellings and the top five stations](/images/30daymapchallenge/2026/day08_utopia_canterbury_bankstown_housing.webp)

## Getting there

My first prompt tried to do this for every LGA in Greater Sydney at once, with exclusions for flooding, bushfire and heritage.
It ran into MapAI's 40-turn limit every time.

So I narrowed it down: one LGA, one exclusion and one simple density rule.

## The prompt

> Utopia scenario for the Canterbury-Bankstown LGA only. Projected unmet housing demand there is 23,600 dwellings by 2041.
>
> 1. Find the railway and metro stations inside Canterbury-Bankstown LGA.
> 2. Select property lots within 800 m of those stations, excluding lots in flood planning areas.
> 3. Assume each lot could hold 6 dwellings per 1,000 m² of lot area, and sum the potential dwellings for each station.
>
> Output 2 layers: the 800 m station catchments, coloured by potential dwellings (light to dark), and the stations as dark dots labelled with their names. Tooltip: station, eligible lots, potential dwellings.
>
> Tell me the total potential dwellings, how that compares with the 23,600 unmet demand, and the top 5 stations. Keep it simple.

The density of 6 dwellings per 1,000 m² is my own assumption, chosen to keep things simple. It's roughly the density of a block of townhouses, not apartment towers.

## What MapAI found

- **18 stations** inside Canterbury-Bankstown.
- **766,524 potential dwellings** across their 800 m catchments.
- That's **3,248% of the 23,600-dwelling shortfall**, more than 32 times what's needed.

The top five stations:

| Station       | Potential dwellings |
| ------------- | ------------------- |
| Bankstown     | 152,334             |
| Canterbury    | 76,166              |
| Campsie       | 60,966              |
| Leightonfield | 53,663              |

## What MapAI did

By the numbers, this map took:

- **32 tool calls**
- **About 8 minutes 20 seconds** (500 seconds) from prompt to finished map
- **About 87 credits**

Here is its evidence trail, in its own words where possible.

### How it understood the question

- **Source:** the Canterbury-Bankstown LGA boundary as the study area.
- **Measure:** potential dwellings equal lot area multiplied by 6 dwellings per 1,000 square metres.
- **Geography:** railway-station points represent the requested railway and metro stations.
- **Threshold:** eligible lots selected within 800 metres of each station.
- **Category:** Flood Planning Area polygons used for the flood exclusion.
- **Assumption:** lots within overlapping station catchments count toward each station's total.
- **Output:** catchment polygons and named station points, with the requested tooltip fields.

### The workflow

1. Verified the Canterbury-Bankstown LGA boundary and identified 18 station points inside it.
2. Excluded property lots intersecting the available Flood Planning Area polygons.
3. Matched eligible lots within 800 metres and calculated potential dwellings for each station.
4. Saved the catchment and station layers with the station metrics.

### The data

All from MapAI's base database:

- **Boundaries** for the LGA.
- **Amenities** for the stations.
- **Properties** for the lots.
- **Hazards** for the flood planning areas.

### The SQL

The core of it is a spatial join from each station to every lot within 800 metres, then a sum of lot area:

```sql
SELECT
  s.station,
  COUNT(el.geometry)::integer AS eligible_lots,
  ROUND(
    (COALESCE(SUM(el.lot_area_m2), 0) * 6.0 / 1000.0)::numeric,
    0
  )::integer AS potential_dwellings
FROM
  stations s
  LEFT JOIN eligible_lots el ON el.geometry && ST_Expand (s.geometry, 800)
  AND ST_DWithin (s.geometry, el.geometry, 800)
GROUP BY
  s.station
```

### Gaps and uncertainties

MapAI flagged three:

- **The flood planning area polygons available did not intersect Canterbury-Bankstown**, so the flood exclusion removed 0 lots.
- **The total adds up overlapping catchments**, so some lots are counted for more than one station.
- **Every eligible lot is treated as developable** at the assumed density.

## What I noticed

The answer is a utopia all right: 32 times the homes Canterbury-Bankstown needs, all within a 10-minute walk of a train.
It's also **too good to be true**, and working out why is the most useful part of today's map.

A quick sanity check shows it.
An 800-metre circle covers about **2 km²**.
Bankstown's 152,334 dwellings, at 6 per 1,000 m², need about **25 km²** of lots.
That's more than 12 times the area of the circle itself.

So where does the extra land come from?
These are my best explanations, not things MapAI confirmed:

- **Whole lots count, not just the part inside the circle.** `ST_DWithin` keeps any lot that touches the 800-metre radius, and then adds its full area. One large parcel, like a rail corridor, a park or an industrial site, that clips the edge of a catchment adds all of its area.
- **Strata lots repeat the same land.** On Day 5, MapAI found that every unit in an apartment block is its own lot with the same footprint. An apartment block with 50 units counts its land 50 times.
- **Overlapping catchments double count.** MapAI flagged this one itself. Along the Bankstown line, where stations are about a kilometre apart, the circles overlap heavily.
- **Nothing is excluded except flooding,** and the flood exclusion did nothing. Canterbury-Bankstown sits on the Cooks and Georges rivers, so I'd expect some flood-affected land. The data available just didn't cover it.

A fairer version would clip each lot to its catchment, collapse strata lots to one footprint, dissolve the overlapping catchments, and leave out roads, parks, rail land and existing apartment blocks.
My guess is the answer would shrink a lot, but it could easily still cover 23,600 dwellings.
After all, that's only about 1,300 homes per station.

That's the lesson of Day 8: the map looks great, the SQL does what it says, and the number is still wrong.
A utopia map is only as believable as its assumptions, and checking them is still the GIS analyst's job.
